Pages at device sizes

Each frame is the exact CSS size of its device, scaled to fit. Panel columns and button size are computed per frame, so what you see is what that device shows.
Scale:

Buttons are square. The side equals the column width, so a 1-wide panel gives a 126 px square. That is what makes the count predictable: a booth screen fits six, a 13″ tablet five, a laptop or 11″ tablet four, a phone in landscape two.

Panels never scroll vertically. A scroll indicator was tried — a chevron, then an illuminated rail with a count — and neither was convincing. The better answer was to remove the case: if the buttons do not fit, the button shrinks and the panel widens until they do.

Size and columns are searched together, not in sequence. For each column count, take the largest button that still shows every row, and use the first arrangement inside the width budget. Doing one and then the other produced panels twice as wide — expanding columns first commits to a shape before finding out a slightly smaller button would have kept the original one.

A panel occupies the same footprint as N channel strips, gaps included. A 2-wide panel is 314 px — two 150 px strips plus the 14 px between them — so the surface reads as one grid rather than two systems sitting next to each other. The slack between the buttons and that width becomes the horizontal gap, which is what stops multi-column panels looking crammed: at full size a 2-wide panel gets 38 px between its columns rather than 12.

Where the slack would be excessive the panel takes its natural width instead. Two 96 px buttons held to a 314 px footprint would sit 98 px apart, which is dead space rather than breathing room. Past 40 px of slack the panel gives up the alignment and uses a 20 px gap — still generous, and honest about the fact that small buttons do not fill a strip-width column.

That keeps the configured shape almost everywhere. The 5-button Stage panel stays one column on every target but a phone in landscape, shrinking from 126 px to 96 px rather than becoming two columns. The venue configured a shape; the layout honours it and adjusts the size.

Only the phone in landscape genuinely runs out. At 260 px of panel height a five-row column would need 42 px buttons, below the 64 px floor — so it becomes 2 × 3 at 78 px. That is the one case where the arrangement changes rather than the size.

One row, always. Strips and panels share a single horizontally scrolling row on every size, including a phone in portrait. Stacking panels beneath the faders was tried and abandoned: it left the faders squashed and the panels half empty, and it meant two layouts to reason about instead of one.

Phone landscape floats the navigation over the surface rather than insetting it, so the fader row keeps its full width. The rail is 56 px of two-character labels, expanding to 176 px on touch and collapsing after about two seconds. With the status bar dropped and compact strip furniture, fader travel goes from an unusable 37 px to 226 px.

Above 1080p the layout should scale, not stretch. Filling the height is right up to a point, and that point arrives sooner than expected: 1080p already gives 201 mm of physical fader travel on a 24″ panel — twice a console fader — and 1440p gives 253 mm. A 4K panel unscaled gives 333 mm, a third of a metre for something moved with one hand, with 126 px buttons stranded beside it.

So a tall display gets a scale factor rather than a longer fader. Everything grows together — strips, type, buttons — and the layout sees a smaller logical viewport. A 4K panel at 2× sees 1920 × 1080 and renders the design target at twice the size, which is what the operating system's own display scaling would do anyway.

The control appears only where it has authority — at or above 1920 × 1080. Below the design target the layout is already choosing button size and column count, and a manual factor would fight that machinery, which is why tablets and phones need no control and do not get one. Viewport size is the test rather than device type, because a booth touchscreen is a coarse pointer and a large display, so it reads as a tablet to every media query that matters.

It opens at a computed default rather than 1.0× — 2.0× on a 4K panel, 1.3× on 1440p, 1.0× on the booth screen — so the slider is for viewing distance and taste, not repair. Range 1.0× to 2.0× in tenths: no downward travel, since below the target the layout owns the problem and going under 1.0 would only push targets past their minimums. Stored per viewport size rather than per browser, so docking a laptop to a monitor recalls each screen's own value.

1920 × 1080 is the design target — the size everything else derives from. Larger displays scale up to it, smaller ones adapt down. The 11″ tablets in landscape are the real floor at 800–834 px tall, not the phone, and they still clear the ~378 px a 100 mm physical fader gives.
